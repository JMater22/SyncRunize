INTRODUCTION
1.1	Project Context
Physical inactivity was a significant public health issue, contributing to 3.2 million deaths annually and ranking among the top ten leading risk factors for global mortality [1]. Running and jogging had long been recognized as essential physical activities that reduce cardiovascular disease risk and overall mortality [2]. However, urban environments present various challenges for runners, including traffic congestion, unsafe road conditions, and insufficient pedestrian-friendly infrastructure, which limited access to safe running spaces.
Pedestrian safety remained a critical issue, with 1.3 million pedestrian deaths occurring annually and pedestrians accounting for approximately 23% of global road fatalities [3]. The risks were particularly pronounced in developing countries, where rapid urbanization had outpaced the development of adequate pedestrian infrastructure. Insufficient sidewalks, poor street lighting, and unregulated road-sharing between vehicles and pedestrians had significantly increased the likelihood of collisions [4]. In Metro Manila, pedestrian fatalities represented 19% of road deaths, second only to motorcyclists, largely due to inadequate road segregation and severe traffic congestion [5]. These unsafe conditions directly affected individuals engaging in outdoor fitness activities, reducing movement efficiency by up to 40% daily [6]. Additionally, crime rates in urban areas, particularly in the Philippines, exacerbated safety concerns. According to data from that period, crime incidents had been steadily increasing, further discouraging outdoor physical activities due to safety risks [7]. 
Given the global rise in smartphone adoption, with over 7 billion active smartphone subscriptions in 2023 [8], mobile application development had become a key enabler across various domains including health, education, and recreation. However, traditional development architectures presented significant drawbacks. Native apps suffered from platform dependency, high development time, and increased maintenance costs due to mobile platform fragmentation [9][10]. Hybrid apps, while offering a single codebase, often compromised performance and user experience due to reliance on browser engines and JavaScript bridges [11][12]. To address these challenges, Progressive Web Apps (PWAs) had emerged as a viable alternative that blended the best of native and web apps, offering features like offline accessibility, background sync, and push notifications without requiring installation [13]. PWAs were particularly suited for applications needing high reach, performance efficiency, and cross-platform compatibility.
Existing fitness applications such as Strava, Nike Run Club, and Adidas Running primarily focused on performance tracking, including distance covered, pace, and calories burned. However, they lacked AI-driven real-time navigation that adapted to traffic and safety conditions. AI-based fitness solutions had shown significant potential in enhancing user experience, with AI-generated personalized training plans improving adherence to fitness goals by 30% [14]. Despite this, no widely available running application incorporated real-time route optimization based on urban traffic conditions.
Furthermore, gamification had emerged as a proven strategy to enhance user engagement in digital fitness solutions. Gamification involves the application of game-like elements such as achievements, challenges, and progress tracking in non-game contexts to increase motivation and adherence [15]. Studies indicated that gamification improves user motivation, yet up to 80% of gamified apps failed due to poor implementation and engagement strategies [16]. This suggested the necessity of well-structured gamification frameworks tailored to fitness applications to sustain long-term behavioral adherence.
Community-based running programs, such as parkrun, had demonstrated positive impacts on physical activity levels, social inclusion, and mental well-being. Individuals from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds and previously inactive populations report significant improvements in health and engagement when participating in structured running initiatives [17]. However, existing community-based running models lacked technological integration that optimized group coordination, enhances route safety, and sustained engagement through gamification mechanisms.
To support the development of the proposed application, the project team had conducted the SyncRunize Capstone Research Survey – Enhancing the Urban and Semi-Urban Running Experience. Among the 14 respondents, 11 had reported experiencing safety issues while running outdoors. The most common concerns included poor street lighting, encounters with stray dogs, and navigating unfamiliar or isolated routes. These safety issues significantly influenced the respondents' running behavior, with 12 having admitted to avoiding specific areas entirely due to past experiences or perceived dangers. This highlighted a strong need for integrated safety features in the SyncRunize application, particularly those addressing route visibility, animal-related hazards, and real-time alerts for potentially unsafe zones.
To address the challenges of urban and semi-urban running safety, the proposed project was designed to optimize routes by analyzing environmental conditions came from crowdsourced reports and crime historical records, ensuring safer running experiences. To enhance the running experience, live traffic was displayed and sent as notification to runners. Gamification features, including personalized challenges and social leaderboards, encouraged users to follow verified safe routes. Additionally, community-driven functionalities enabled route-sharing, group coordination, and collaborative hazard reporting. Voice navigation provided real-time and hands-free guidance. The app was developed using the Progressive Web App (PWA) approach to ensure accessibility and performance across various devices. 
The proposed project used prescriptive hazard-aware pathfinding system. Moreover, the route generated was fixed during runtime, relying solely on crowdsourced hazard reports and verified historical crime records. The system assessed hazard credibility using Trust Score and Agreement Score models, which collectively inform risk scoring for route computation. Real-time updates were not used for dynamic rerouting, but critical new hazard reports with high agreement scores were immediately pushed to the user as notifications.
Natural Language Processing (NLP) was employed to facilitate hands-free voice input and descriptive assistance, using Sentence-BERT for semantic understanding. The routing component used modified Dijkstra algorithm tailored to include risk-based edge weights, not machine learning predictions. This structured yet flexible integration of descriptive and prescriptive components defined the uniqueness of the system.

	Purpose and Description
The purpose of this project was to design and develop a Progressive Web Application that optimizes route selection for urban and semi-urban runners by addressing safety risks, traffic congestion, and inefficient navigation. Unlike conventional fitness applications that focused primarily on performance tracking, the proposed system integrated real-time route recommendations based on other runners’ route history, crowdsourced hazard reporting, and safe navigation to enhance user safety and route efficiency. Additionally, gamification and community collaboration encouraged consistent physical activity and safer running habits.
The system was designed to leverage traffic data from Google Maps and integrate user-submitted hazard reports to support route optimization. To supplement safety assessments, selected records from the Tarlac Police Provincial Office were analyzed to identify high-risk areas. These datasets, combined with community-contributed insights on poorly lit areas, unsafe zones, and obstructions, enabled the AI to adjust and improve route recommendations dynamically.
A core feature of the system was AI-powered voice navigation, which provided real-time guidance, and safety notifications based on environmental conditions. The AI continuously learned from user feedback, traffic patterns, and reported hazards to improve safety predictions and route accuracy over time.
The benefactors of this system were the following:
For Urban Runners - The system was designed to provide urban runners with AI-driven route optimization, real-time navigation, and safety enhancements to minimize risks such as accidents, congestion, and road hazards.

For Fitness Enthusiasts - Gamification elements, including personalized challenges, milestone achievements, and social leaderboards, were implemented to enhance motivation and engagement, encouraging consistent running habits while prioritizing safe routes.

For Running Communities - The platform was developed to foster community-based collaboration, allowing users to coordinate group runs, share optimized routes, and contribute to real-time safety reporting, strengthening social connections and collective safety.

For City Planners and Local Authorities - Aggregated data on pedestrian movement, congestion patterns, and safety reports was designed to support urban planning and public safety initiatives by identifying high-risk areas and improving pedestrian infrastructure.

1.4	Scope and Limitations
The proposed project involved the design, development, and evaluation of SyncRunize, a mobile-first Progressive Web Application (PWA) that was designed to promote safe, data-informed, and community-driven running experiences within Tarlac Province and Tarlac City. Developed by students of the Bachelor of Science in Information Technology – Web and Mobile Application (BSIT-WMA) program at Tarlac State University, the system adopted a user-centered design approach to meet the real-world needs of urban and semi-urban runners. It was designed to accommodate varying road conditions, network limitations, and route complexities, ensuring inclusivity and accessibility within the specified geographic area.
The application featured core modules such as user authentication, which supported both traditional email-password logins and social login mechanisms to ease account creation and access. Once authenticated, users were directed to a dashboard, which displayed personalized progress statistics, activity feeds, challenge invitations, and leaderboard rankings. The route module included route generation which used a prescriptive pathfinding algorithm that computed routes based on user-defined start point and distance, incorporating hazard risks from trust and agreement scores. The system did not perform adaptive or real-time rerouting.
During live runs, the application provided real-time GPS tracking to monitor user location, offered turn-by-turn navigation, and delivered safety-related notifications through audio prompts. A voice assistant feature was implemented to support hands-free guidance during active sessions. This assistant functioned in English and did not offer multilingual support in the initial release. The voice assistant supported hands-free interaction with descriptive input and output only and did not affect route generation or rerouting. It was primarily used for hands-free interaction and hazard description entry. Runners were empowered to report hazards encountered along their routes, including but not limited to blocked roads, damaged sidewalks, slippery terrain, and poorly lit areas. These reports populated a crowdsourced hazard map, forming the core of the system’s safety intelligence.
To improve the credibility of hazard reports, the system implemented a scoring model that combined a user’s Trust Score and each report’s Agreement Score, ensuring only credible data contributed to route risk evaluation. This scoring model assigned safety levels to route segments based on user reliability and corroboration, as determined by Trust and Agreement Scores. Additionally, a user trust scoring model was used to weigh reports from users with consistent and validated reporting histories more heavily than those from new or unverified contributors.
The system used historical safety data provided by the Tarlac Police Provincial Office as a supplementary reference to identify high-risk areas. However, due to the absence of a live crime data API, this data was used only for route scoring and was not updated in real time, covering the period from January 2025 to one month prior to system deployment. This data served to identify historically problematic areas but did not directly influence real-time route generation. Its role was limited to providing general context for the development team during system configuration and initial hazard mapping.
A community module was also included to enhance user engagement. This module allowed runners to create or join challenges, share completed routes, follow other users, and interact with a leaderboard system. The system included performance analytics tools, enabling users to review running statistics, identify trends, and plan future sessions. Users also had access to basic privacy settings to control the visibility of their activity within the app.
The system was developed as a mobile-first PWA using Ionic Framework with React for frontend development and was wrapped using Capacitor to enable access to native device features, especially on iOS. This approach allowed the system to maintain the scalability and responsiveness of a web-based platform while gaining access to advanced functionalities such as background geolocation, continuous GPS tracking, and speech synthesis—all of which were not fully supported in traditional browser-based environments. On Android, users were able to install the application through browser prompts, while on iOS, installation was done through Safari’s “Add to Home Screen” functionality. However, for full functionality—especially background GPS tracking and real-time voice updates—the system required insta0llation via a Capacitor-wrapped mobile app on both platforms.
The desktop version of SyncRunize provided limited access to non-real-time features such as account management, challenge creation, leaderboard browsing, and route planning. However, run tracking and background sensor features were disabled on desktop due to the absence of continuous geolocation support and limitations in accessing system-level sensors. Users attempting to access these modules via a desktop browser were prompted to switch to a supported mobile device. Similarly, users accessing the system via a standard mobile browser were encouraged to download the full application for complete access to features such as live tracking, hazard detection, and voice-guided navigation.
The application supported mobile devices running Android version 10 and above and iOS version 15 and above, requiring a minimum of 4 GB RAM, GPS capability, and a modern browser with PWA support enabled. For desktop users, the system was accessible through the latest versions of Chrome, Safari, Firefox, or Edge on Windows 10 and above and macOS Monterey and above. Despite this broad platform compatibility, real-time functionalities remained restricted to mobile environments with sufficient sensor and API access.
The system’s biometric health feature was limited to calorie burned estimation based on route distance and basic user data. It did not include other biometric tracking such as heart rate or oxygen level monitoring. Moreover, the application relied heavily on third-party services such as the Google Maps Platform, which could introduce occasional limitations such as inaccuracies in route rendering, delays due to API quotas, or restricted pedestrian-specific data in certain areas. A stable internet connection was required to access essential features, including live GPS tracking, prescriptive route display, voice assistant responses, and real-time hazard notifications. Offline functionality was limited to viewing previously cached data, such as saved routes and activity summaries.
The system’s performance and usability were validated through black-box testing. Evaluation included internal testing by TSU BSIT-WMA students, field testing by local urban and semi-urban runners, and technical review by faculty advisers. For the initial deployment phase, the system required at least ten pre-seeded hazard reports, twenty user-submitted entries, and five well-defined hazard categories to ensure a functional hazard mapping experience. Five user-created and system-generated challenges were also seeded to validate the social module. These metrics supported minimum viable validation of the hazard scoring and community engagement systems. The geographical coverage remained limited to Tarlac City and Tarlac Province during testing and evaluation, allowing the development team to monitor data flow, user behavior, and system performance in a controlled setting.

Agreement Score (ASⱼ)
In hazard-aware systems, Agreement Scoring (AS) was used to evaluate the reliability of individual hazard reports by measuring their similarity to other nearby reports in both space and time. It was based on the intuition that hazards were more credible if multiple users reported similar incidents near the same location within a reasonable time window.
Unlike real-time navigation platforms, which typically relied on high-frequency updates and short windows (e.g., 30–60 minutes), our approach supported low-frequency environments where users might report hazards hours or days apart. Inspired by techniques in Lozano Domínguez & Sanguino [36], we extended the temporal window for agreement to 7 days, enabling delayed corroboration while applying exponential decay to favor recent data.

The system applied a structured similarity scoring matrix to quantify the degree of alignment between hazard types. This supported environments with multiple hazard categories such as potholes, poor lighting, and structural damage.

This hybrid scoring approach enabled the system to balance structured taxonomies with the flexibility of natural language descriptions, which is essential in dynamic, user-driven reporting systems.

Developing a Progressive Web Application (PWA) for urban and semi-urban runners required an understanding of existing navigation systems, gamification techniques, safety reporting mechanisms, and PWA development frameworks. Prior research had explored various aspects of these domains, but notable gaps remained in integrating real-time traffic-aware, safe route generation, crowdsourced safety alerts, and AI-powered voice-assisted navigation in a unified system.
Gamification had been studied extensively in fitness applications, primarily to enhance user engagement. Khan [18] developed a mobile fitness game prototype that incorporated game mechanics and personalized motivation strategies. While the study demonstrated the effectiveness of gamification in promoting user participation, its limited testing scope and incomplete feature implementation suggested a need for further validation in real-world fitness applications. Additionally, the study did not address navigation safety concerns, a crucial factor for outdoor running applications.
Navigation optimization had evolved beyond traditional shortest-path algorithms to consider factors such as safety and environmental conditions. Siriaraya et al. [20] proposed a taxonomy-based classification of pedestrian navigation systems, including safety-aware routing. However, their study lacked a standardized framework, making it difficult to compare the effectiveness of different approaches. Complementing this work, Lin and Lin [22] empirically identified two dominant navigation strategies: 58% of users favoring direction cues (e.g., compass/blue beam) for efficiency and 42% preferring landmarks for environmental grounding. This study's focus on university students and exclusion of real-time hazard scenarios presented an opportunity to extend these findings to diverse runner populations in dynamic environments.
Crowdsourced safety reporting improved situational awareness in navigation systems. Hamilton et al. [24] developed Transafe, a platform aggregating crime and safety reports from users. The system generated real-time alerts, though potential reporting biases may impact data reliability. Alpkoçak and Cetin [27] created a safety-aware routing system using a modified A* algorithm with crime rate heuristics. This method calculated routes minimizing high-crime area exposure. The implementation currently excluded environmental factors and real-time traffic data, limiting route optimization capabilities.
The development of Progressive Web Applications (PWAs) had gained traction due to their cross-platform accessibility and offline functionality. Muawwal [21] analyzed PWA implementation and its impact on mobile accessibility and website performance. The study highlighted the advantages of PWAs, including improved loading speed and user experience, but focused primarily on Express.js, limiting insights into PWA performance across different frameworks. Adetunji et al. [23] further compared mobile development architectures, concluding that PWAs offered significant benefits over native and hybrid applications. However, the study did not extensively discuss barriers to PWA adoption, particularly in integrating AI-driven features for fitness applications.
Community-driven running applications had demonstrated their potential in fostering engagement and motivation among users. Yang et al. [25] examined the influence of social interaction on running behavior, emphasizing the role of group dynamics in sustaining fitness motivation. While the study provided insights into social engagement, it did not explore AI-driven personalization techniques that could enhance user experience in individual and group running scenarios. Similarly, Quirk et al. [17] investigated the impact of community-based running programs on physical activity levels. The research supported the benefits of social support in fitness applications but lacked an implementation framework that integrates safe route planning and gamified engagement.
Existing studies provided a foundation for navigation-based fitness applications but did not comprehensively address real-time, safe route optimization, AI-powered voice navigation, and integrated hazard reporting. While prior research had examined gamification, safety-aware routing, and PWA development independently, the lack of a unified system that integrated these features represents a gap. This study aimed to bridge that gap by developing a PWA-based AI-integrated running application that leveraged real-time traffic data, crowdsourced hazard reports, and gamification to enhance user experience and ensure safer running routes.

Definition of Terms
The key terminologies and definitions used in this study were outlined in this section for clarity:
Voice Assistant (NLP-Powered) - Referred to a speech-based interface integrated into the system using Natural Language Processing (NLP). It allowed users to provide hazard descriptions and receive verbal feedback. It did not influence route generation or decision-making.
Crowdsourced Report – Referred to data or reports gathered from a group of individuals, often users or community members, contributing real-time information about safety hazards, road conditions, or other relevant factors.
Gamification – Referred to the use of game-like elements, such as leaderboards, challenges, and rewards, in non-game contexts to boost user engagement, motivation, and participation.
Geolocation – Referred to determining a user’s real-time location using GPS or network signals. In SyncRunize, it supported route tracking, hazard localization, and personalized safety alerts based on user position.
Navigation – Referred to the planned, fixed route generation process based on predefined inputs (start point and distance). In this system, it does not adapt during a run but notified users of hazards that arise in real-time.
PWA (Progressive Web Application) – Referred to a mobile-first web application that combined features of native and web apps. It supported offline access, background tracking, and installation outside app stores, with access to device features through wrappers like Capacitor.
Route – Referred to a system-generated path based on safety risk scores, distance, and start point. Routes were generated prescriptively and did not update dynamically once initiated.
Urban – Referred to areas with high population density, developed infrastructure, and active pedestrian and vehicular movement. In this study, urban areas were the primary focus for route generation and hazard mapping due to higher activity and risk density.
Semi Urban – Referred to areas that fall between urban and rural regions, characterized by a mix of urban infrastructure and rural settings, with moderate population density and a combination of residential and commercial areas.
Trust Score - A score assigned to a user based on the reliability, frequency, and consistency of their submitted hazard reports.
Agreement Score - A credibility score for a report, calculated from its similarity to other reports nearby in time and location.
Semantic Similarity - A method for comparing hazard reports using meaning-based matching (e.g., using Sentence-BERT embeddings).
Prescriptive Routing - A pathfinding approach where routes were generated once using fixed data, not updated dynamically during the run.
Calorie Estimation - A system feature that provided users with an approximate number of calories burned during a route, based on distance and personal metrics.
Modified Dijkstra Algorithm - A version of the traditional Dijkstra algorithm adapted to compute the safest—not shortest—route by considering not just distance, but also safety risk scores. In SyncRunize, edge weights were adjusted using hazard risk derived from Trust Scores, Agreement Scores, and crime severity levels. This allowed the algorithm to prioritize lower-risk paths over merely faster or shorter ones.

Sources of Data
The system primarily relied on geospatial inputs, historical datasets, and crowdsourced hazard reports to support prescriptive route generation and real-time hazard notifications. Google Maps API was used to obtain base map data and road network information for computing running paths. Although the platform provided traffic-related data, this information was only utilized as supplementary input to help runners avoid traffic efficiently; however, it was not considered in the algorithm’s evaluation of route safetyInstead, OpenStreetMap (OSM) served as the primary source for identifying the safest paths, as it provided comprehensive road network information and additional environmental context such as sidewalk width, surface type, street lighting, and elevation. These elements were directly incorporated into the route safety evaluation, while Google Maps was primarily used to supply real-time traffic conditions.
A significant portion of safety-related data originated from user-submitted hazard reports. These reports are crowdsourced through the application and include details such as GPS coordinates, hazard type, and time of submission. The reports were stored in Supabase and assessed using a Trust Score model, which evaluated the reliability of the reporting user based on their history and behavior, and an Agreement Score model, which measured the degree of corroboration between similar reports submitted by different users based on time, location, and semantic similarity. These two scores are combined to determine the hazard risk weight associated with each report, which in turn influenced the safety evaluation of route segments.
In addition to user reports, the system integrated historical crime incident records sourced from the Tarlac Police Provincial Office. These crime reports were used as a supplementary dataset to identify areas that have exhibited higher levels of risk over time. Due to the lack of a live data feed or public API access, these crime records were static and used solely for reference in the prescriptive route generation process.
To compute the safest route, the system implemented a Modified Dijkstra Algorithm. This version of Dijkstra did not simply calculate the shortest path but rather incorporated hazard and crime risk into its edge weight calculation. Each road segment or edge in the network is assigned a cost that reflected not only distance but also safety, with values adjusted using hazard risk derived from the product of Trust and Agreement Scores, along with crime severity weights based on the type of incident. This enabled the system to prioritize safer paths over shorter or faster alternatives.
While the generated route remained fixed during the run, the system allowed for real-time hazard alerts. If a new hazard report was submitted and determined to have a high Agreement Score, the runner received a notification, but the system did not dynamically reroute or regenerate the path in response.
For system evaluation, the development adhered to the ISO 25010 software quality model. Usability was assessed based on user feedback, ensuring that features such as report submission and route viewing were intuitive and accessible. Performance efficiency was measured by the responsiveness of the system when generating paths and processing hazard data. Reliability was evaluated through consistent output and stable performance across different devices and conditions. Security was ensured through secure login mechanisms and proper handling of user data. Finally, safety was measured by the system's effectiveness in guiding users through routes that minimize exposure to high-risk areas, based on both community-sourced and historical data.

Algorithm Data Sources and Testing Rationale
The algorithms that were used in this system—specifically the Trust Score model, Agreement Score model, and the Modified Dijkstra Algorithm—required structured input data for calibration, testing, and simulation. These datasets were separate from those gathered via surveys or internet-based information searches.
For the Trust Score model, sample user behavior data was simulated or derived from controlled testing groups, wherein users submitted hazard reports with varying levels of consistency and accuracy. The purpose was to emulate real-world participation patterns in order to evaluate how trust evolves over time based on accuracy and decay functions.
The Agreement Score model required hazard reports clustered by location, time, and similarity. These were based on crowdsourced data collected from real users of the system during testing and seeded with sample reports. To simulate agreement behavior during initial testing, artificial clusters were generated using patterns observed in platforms such as Strava, where users often report events in similar time/place clusters (e.g., during peak running hours). This grouping rationale helped establish realistic baselines for evaluating agreement score effectiveness.
For the Modified Dijkstra Algorithm, the necessary road network data was obtained from Google Maps and OpenStreetMap (OSM). Route testing used both manually created hazard scenarios and real hazard reports that were submitted during testing. Historical crime records that were provided by the Tarlac Police Provincial Office supplemented hazard scoring to reflect actual risk distribution within the route network.

All datasets were stored and managed using Supabase during development and evaluation. These data sources and simulation strategies were necessary to ensure each algorithm functions correctly prior to full-scale deployment.

4.2.5 	Safe Route Pathfinding Algorithm
This section presented the methods that were used to assess hazard report reliability and compute the safest path through a modified Dijkstra algorithm. The overall process incorporated trust scoring, agreement scoring, semantic similarity, and historical crime severity weighting.
Trust-Based Hazard Filtering
To assess the credibility of each user, a Trust Score (Tᵢ) was assigned based on their historical reporting behavior. The score was influenced by:
Reporting Accuracy (Aᵢ): Proportion of user reports that were supported by others.
Temporal Consistency: A decay function reduced the impact of older reports to prioritize recent, relevant data.
Validation Match: Verified or consistently corroborated reports increased the user’s trustworthiness over time.
This trust score was later used to weigh the influence of each report during hazard scoring.

System Evaluation Method
The system evaluation method for the proposed SyncRunize application adhered to the criteria established by the ISO/IEC 25010 standard for software product quality. The key characteristics that were evaluated were usability, performance efficiency, reliability, security, and safety. Each characteristic was evaluated through structured methods and free/open-source tools to ensure systematic and measurable results.
Usability was evaluated using a standardized questionnaire distributed via Google Forms, with responses automatically stored in Google Sheets for structured analysis. The assessment measured navigation clarity, task execution efficiency, and user satisfaction, ensuring that the interface supported accessible and effective interactions.
Performance efficiency was assessed through a combination of browser-based and external testing tools. Chrome DevTools monitored runtime resources and load profiling, while Google Lighthouse provided performance scores, accessibility checks, and optimization insights. To evaluate backend scalability, k6, an open-source load testing tool, simulated stress and load scenarios, identifying latency issues and throughput consistency.
Reliability was tested by executing repeated system operations to verify uptime, stability, and output consistency. Firebase Crashlytics tracked mobile app crashes, while Sentry monitored web and mobile errors. Automated testing frameworks such as Cypress and Playwright ensured repeatable execution of core flows, confirming consistent results across multiple sessions.
Security evaluation focused on authentication, access control, and vulnerability scanning. OWASP ZAP and Burp Suite CE were used for dynamic application security testing (DAST). Dependency scanning tools such as Trivy and OWASP Dependency-Check identified risks in backend libraries, while SonarQube CE provided static code analysis for detecting security hotspots.
Safety evaluation targeted the system’s hazard-related notifications and route guidance. Testing was conducted with peer/volunteer runners to simulate authentic usage. Structured feedback was collected through Google Forms, while the GPS Emulator in Android Studio was used to simulate routes and trigger hazard alerts in controlled environments, confirming timely and accurate alert delivery.
All evaluation outcomes were documented and analyzed to confirm system alignment with the selected ISO/IEC 25010 quality characteristics. This structured approach ensured that SyncRunize achieved its intended objectives in terms of usability, performance efficiency, reliability, security, and safety, providing a reliable and user-centered solution for its target users.
